# 23 — The competition slide: what works, what VCs want (as of 2026-10-08)

**Scope.** This note covers how strong seed to Series B decks present competition, and what investors say about that slide. It closes with guidance for crypto and AI-agent companies, where the field is crowded and changes month to month. It builds on notes 07, 11, 16–22 and `../vc-review.md`.

**Method.**
- I ran about 45 WebSearch queries in extended mode.
- WebFetch could not resolve sequoiacap.com or ycombinator.com (ENOTFOUND), and curl got a 403 from the egress proxy. **So every web fact here comes from a search-result extract and is tagged (s).**
- Many deck "teardowns" come from template or design vendors. They often disagree on slide numbers, slide counts and lead investors. Conflicts are flagged inline, and **?** marks a claim I could not pin down.
- Check any quote against its original before it goes on a slide or into a memo.

---

## 0. TL;DR

- **The slide is a credibility check.** It rarely wins a round, but it often loses one. DocSend's 2024 seed data shows investor attention on competition **fell 48% year on year**, while attention on Team rose 40% ([PR Newswire/DocSend](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/vcs-prioritize-people-in-an-ai-heavy-landscape-according-to-new-dropbox-docsend-report-302334444.html) (s)). Investors spend about 2.5 minutes on the whole deck, so this slide gets seconds. The claim has to be readable at a glance.
- **Sequoia's whole brief for the slide** is: "Who are your direct and indirect competitors. Show that you have a plan to win." ([Sequoia on Medium](https://medium.com/sequoia-capital/writing-a-business-plan-ea378b50abe2) (s))
- **YC does not require a competition slide.** Seibel's "How to Pitch Your Company" asks for a *unique insight* instead: "what you know about the problem that everyone else doesn't" ([YC Library](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4b-how-to-pitch-your-company) (s); [Stripe Atlas](https://stripe.com/guides/atlas/pitching) (s)).
- **In a crowded, fast field, structure beats features.** The formats that hold up show a structural trade-off, the status quo, and one reason rivals can't or won't follow. The weakest format is the self-graded feature grid.

## 1. What the slide is for

| Source | What it says the slide must do |
|---|---|
| **Sequoia** (business plan template) | "Competition / alternatives: Who are your direct and indirect competitors. Show that you have a plan to win." ([Medium](https://medium.com/sequoia-capital/writing-a-business-plan-ea378b50abe2) (s)). The older SlideShare copy reduces it to "List competitors · List competitive advantages" ([SlideShare](https://www.slideshare.net/mobile/PitchDeckCoach/sequoia-capital-pitchdecktemplate) (s)). |
| **YC: Seibel / Harris** | Seibel's seven questions include "What's your unique insight?" and leave out "who are your competitors" ([YC Library](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4b-how-to-pitch-your-company) (s)). A secondary summary lists Aaron Harris's seed template as Problem → Solution → Traction → Revenue → Business model → Market → Team, with no competition slide ([LinkedIn summary](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/how-create-perfect-pitch-deck-matt-estes) (s)) **?**. Stripe Atlas's pitching guide, written with Seibel's input, says to acknowledge competitors, because having none implies an unattractive market or no research. It also says to explain how you differ and what that difference wins you ([Stripe Atlas](https://stripe.com/guides/atlas/pitching) (s)). |
| **Guy Kawasaki** (Slide 7) | Show "how and why you can beat the competition", meaning what you can do that it can't. Also "provide information about your weaknesses vis-à-vis the competition" ([guykawasaki.com](https://guykawasaki.com/how-to-create-an-enchanting-pitch-officeandguyk/) (s)). |
| **Reid Hoffman** (LinkedIn Series B, slide 14) | "Express your competitive advantage." Investors won't believe you have an edge unless you state it clearly and decisively ([reidhoffman.org](https://www.reidhoffman.org/linkedin-pitch-to-greylock/) (s)). |
| **NFX** | The deck "must have good competitive analysis." Every slide headline should state the takeaway, so a slide titled "Competitors" wastes the space. "Memorize the competitive landscape" ([NFX 16 lessons](https://www.nfx.com/post/16-fundraising-lessons) (s); [NFX Fundraising Manual](https://www.nfx.com/post/nfx-fundraising-manual) (s)). |
| **Mark Suster** | Use the slide to show your relative position, and where you need capital to catch up ([Both Sides](https://medium.com/both-sides-of-the-table/how-to-talk-to-investors-about-your-competitors-fe1ec8d59c42) (s)). |
| **Raymond Luk** | The slide has to close three gaps: "different does not equal better, better does not necessarily capture market share, and capturing market share does not mean you can defend it." Keep asking "so what?" ([raymondluk.co](https://www.raymondluk.co/p/how-not-to-lie-to-yourself-about) (s)). |
| **April Dunford** | Start from *competitive alternatives*, meaning what a best-fit buyer would do if you didn't exist. "The first one to beat is the status quo" ([aprildunford.com](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/positioning-and-competition) (s)). |

### What DocSend's data does and doesn't show
- **The 2015 baseline.** Tom Eisenmann and DocSend studied 200 startups that raised $360M. The average time on a deck was 3:44 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2015/06/08/lessons-from-a-study-of-perfect-pitch-decks-vcs-spend-an-average-of-3-minutes-44-seconds-on-them/) (s)).
- **Time per deck since then.** It fell to 2:24 in Q4 2023 and was about 2:30 in 2024 ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/investor-activity-surpasses-2021-engagement-levels-hits-q1-record-high-according-to-docsend-2024-data-302113696.html) (s)).
- **2023 seed report (170 decks).** Investors "sharpened their focus on sections like Competition and Why now?" Why Now got 65% more time, even though total time per deck fell 20% ([Nasdaq/PR](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/why-now:-successful-founders-display-urgency-among-market-competition-in-docsends) (s)).
- **2024 seed report.** Time on the Competition slide fell 48% year on year. Team got 40% more time at seed and 30% more at pre-seed ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/vcs-prioritize-people-in-an-ai-heavy-landscape-according-to-new-dropbox-docsend-report-302334444.html) (s)).
- **Per-slide seconds.** Secondary sites quote 22s or 34s for the competition slide. They conflict, so don't use either **?**.
- **What I did not find:** any DocSend data linking a particular *competition format* to getting funded. One site claims "51% more time on the Competition slide in successful decks", but gives no source. Treat it as unverified.

**So:** competition is a hygiene slide whose weight swings with the market. In a crowded year it gets scrutiny, and otherwise it gets skimmed. Either way it is read in seconds, so the claim belongs in the headline.

## 2. Seven proven formats

There is no consensus on format.
- **Meta-review of 20 expert sources.** The 2×2 ("Magic Quadrant") was cited most, at 8 for, 3 against and 8 neutral. Harvey balls came next ([STORY](https://www.storypitchdecks.com/post/whats-the-most-effective-visual-for-your-competition-slide-we-studied-opinions-from-20-experts) (s)). That page's own totals don't add up.
- **Underscore VC** rates the 2×2 best. It says a Venn diagram "doesn't show why you'll win", and a feature matrix "often simply turns into a long list of features" ([Underscore](https://underscore.vc/startupsecrets/competitive-landscape-slide/) (s)).
- **Extantia Capital** says nearly every deck it sees uses a Magic Quadrant or a power grid ([Medium](https://medium.com/extantia-capital/get-your-competition-slide-right-2b1621eabe86) (s)).

### F1. 2×2 positioning map
- **What it is.** Two axes, with you alone in one quadrant.
- **When it works.**
  - The axes are **trade-offs buyers already make**, and rivals would accept the axis labels.
  - The empty quadrant is empty for a *structural* reason: a different business model, cost base or customer.
  - It reads in 3 seconds, which suits a skimmed slide.
- **When it backfires.**
  - The axes were reverse-engineered so you land top-right. "An axis whose label describes your product rather than a market trade-off" is the tell ([Deckary](https://deckary.com/blog/competitive-landscape-slide) (s)).
  - In crowded categories "every company places itself in the upper-right" ([Understory](https://www.understoryagency.com/blog/competitor-analysis-pitch-deck-guide) (s)).
  - Dreamit Ventures warns that it squeezes your edge into two dimensions ([Deckary](https://deckary.com/blog/competitive-landscape-slide) (s)).
- **Example decks.**
  - **Airbnb (2008/09 seed).** Price (affordable↔expensive) × transaction (offline↔online). Hotels and VRBO sit in expensive/online, and Craigslist and Couchsurfing in affordable/offline. Teardowns disagree on where Couchsurfing and Hostels.com sit ([Spectup](https://www.spectup.com/resource-hub/airbnb-pitch-deck-analysis) (s); [startupfundraising.com](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/airbnb-redesigned-pitch-deck-teardown) (s)).
  - **Front (2016 Series A, $10M led by Social Capital).** "Messaging experience" (bad↔good) × "business-ready" (consumer↔business). Zendesk, Hootsuite and Salesforce Desk are business-ready but poor messaging; Gmail, Outlook, Messenger and Polymail are good messaging but consumer. Front is alone top-right. Another teardown describes a different, grouped layout **?** ([startupfundraising.com](https://startupfundraising.com/library/pitch-deck-examples/slides/competition-slide/by-stage) (s); [Collin on Medium](https://collinmathilde.medium.com/front-series-a-deck-f2e2775a419b) (s)).
  - **Fivetran (later stage).** Incumbent↔startup × custom ETL↔automated ELT. Both axes are real architecture and vendor-type trade-offs (same source (s)).
- **Hunter Walk's test.** Ask what the rivals on the chart would say about its labels and their own placement. He has "never been presented one of these slides where the startup pitching isn't in the 'upper right' square" ([hunterwalk.com](https://hunterwalk.com/2020/05/25/if-your-pitch-deck-has-a-competitive-2x2-im-going-to-ask-you-this-question/) (s)).

### F2. Status quo: what customers do today
- **What it is.** You compete against the workaround, not against logos. Dunford: "What would your best customers do if your product didn't exist?" Often the answer is Excel, an intern, or doing nothing ([aprildunford.com](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/positioning-and-competition) (s)).
- **When it works.**
  - A new behavior or category.
  - Pre-seed and seed, where direct rivals are few.
  - When most lost deals go to "no decision". Dunford's figures for this conflict: one post says the status quo wins "one deal in four", a summary says ~40% **?**.
- **When it backfires.** When obvious direct rivals exist and you leave them out, it reads as evasion. That is especially true at Series A and later, where investors expect "category dominance, not just competitive parity" ([Understory](https://www.understoryagency.com/blog/competitor-analysis-pitch-deck-guide) (s)).
- **Example decks.**
  - **Coinbase (2012 seed).** No dedicated competition slide. Its "Why Coinbase will win" slide shows how people held bitcoin *without* Coinbase: "ugly software full of long cryptographic keys" ([Tremendous blog](https://tremendous.blog/2024/02/28/the-coinbase-deck/) (s); [startupfundraising.com](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/coinbase-round-pitch-deck-teardown) (s)).
  - **Uber (2008).** "Cabs in 2008": radio dispatch, 14 mpg Crown Victorias, hailing by hand, no GPS. A "User Benefits" slide compares UberCab with taxis and limos. There is no 2×2 ([startupfundraising.com](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/uber-pitch-deck-teardown) (s)).
  - **Brex.** Reportedly no competition slide. It showed **actual card rejection letters from Capital One, Amex and Chase**, and contrasted underwriting on cash and funding with underwriting on credit history ([Upmetrics](https://upmetrics.co/pitch-deck-examples/brex) (s)).
- **Tip.** Put the status quo in *every* format, as a column or a row. Investors dislike slides that ignore indirect players ([Spectup](https://www.spectup.com/resource-hub/competition-slide-pitch-deck) (s)).

### F3. Feature matrix: checkmark grid, Harvey balls or power grid
- **What it is.** Rows are rivals, columns are capabilities.
- **When it works.**
  - Series A or B in a *defined* category with 3–5 named rivals the investor already knows.
  - The columns are **buying criteria** (what buyers weigh), not things you happen to build.
  - Marks are honest and partial (Harvey balls), and rivals' strengths are visible.
  - Suster endorses "two-by-two matrix or Harvey Balls which establish your relative position" ([Both Sides](https://medium.com/both-sides-of-the-table/how-to-talk-to-investors-about-your-competitors-fe1ec8d59c42) (s)).
  - It is very useful as an **appendix or diligence** slide.
- **When it backfires.** This is the most-criticized format.
  - Suster: "with some money and resources, every competitor could replicate your oh-so-great features" (s).
  - Extantia: founders "choose a biased feature list so their product looks like the clear winner. Everyone in the room knows that isn't how the market really works" (s).
  - "Every checkmark is something a competitor could add in a quarter" ([Understory](https://www.understoryagency.com/blog/competitor-analysis-pitch-deck-guide) (s)).
- **Example decks.**
  - **Artisan (2025 Series A, $25M, AI SDR agents).** A matrix against four *unnamed* competitors ([startupfundraising.com](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/artisan-series-a-pitch-deck-teardown) (s), unverified against the [BI original](https://www.businessinsider.com/pitch-deck-ai-agent-startup-artisan-series-a-funding-round-2025-4)).
  - **Tocker's own "spotlight matrix"** (`docs/pitch/deck/build_competition.py`) uses this format. The internal partner review called it "not credible": the grid is self-graded, and "x402 data" is "a feature you chose, not a buyer need" (`../vc-review.md`).

### F4. Landscape or category map: grouped logos, fragmentation, hub
- **What it is.** Rivals grouped by approach or segment, to show a gap or fragmentation.
- **When it works.**
  - "Fragmented today → we unify" stories, or when the *gap itself* is the insight.
  - Crowded fields where you must prove you know everyone. Group them, don't list them.
- **When it backfires.**
  - "Logo soup" with no analysis "tells investors nothing about why your product wins" ([SlideGenius](https://www.slidegenius.com/cm-faq-question/what-is-the-importance-of-including-a-competition-slide-in-a-pitch-deck) (s)).
  - Maps go stale in weeks in AI ([operator.blog](https://operator.blog/2025/03/05/market-maps-junior-vc-life/) (s)).
  - A Venn or map "doesn't show why you'll win" (Underscore (s)).
- **Example decks.**
  - **Intercom (2011 seed, $600k).** Five fragmented categories (social, support/Zendesk, email/MailChimp, in-app, analytics/Mixpanel) with no integrated player. Teardowns disagree on the exact groups ([startupfundraising.com](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/intercom-1m-vc-investment-turned-into-1-3b-intercom-s-initia-pitch-deck-teardown) (s); [Intercom blog](https://www.intercom.com/blog/first-pitch-deck/) (s)).
  - **Notion (later deck).** Red-circled logos of Dropbox, Evernote, Trello, Quip, Confluence, Jira and Google Docs, then Notion as the hub they feed into ([startupfundraising.com](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/notion-pitch-deck-teardown) (s)).
  - **Figma (2013 seed).** Adobe atop a pyramid with small tools below. The deck was mostly a live demo ([startupfundraising.com](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/figma-pitch-deck-teardown) (s)).

### F5. Comparison to the analog incumbent, and counter-positioning
- **What it is.** You against the legacy way the job gets done, showing *why the incumbent can't copy you without hurting itself*.
- **The underlying idea.** Hamilton Helmer's counter-positioning: a new model that is better for customers, where "for an incumbent to mimic it would cause them serious damage" ([NFX 7 Powers](https://www.nfx.com/post/seven-powers) (s)).
- **When it works.** The incumbent's profit pool depends on the very thing you remove, such as a fee model, custody, KYC or per-seat pricing.
- **When it backfires.**
  - The analog is not what *your* buyer actually uses today.
  - The incumbent can copy you cheaply.
  - The "modest superiority" point: too big a gap provokes a response (Helmer, via [notes](https://marginalfutility.substack.com/p/13-counter-positioning-as-a-business) (s)).
- **Example decks.**
  - **Uber** vs taxis and limos, and **Airbnb** vs hotels (above).
  - **Brex** vs bank and Amex underwriting (above).
  - **Crypto analogs.** Coinbase vs self-managed keys. Phantom chose Solana, "where MetaMask … does not play" ([Bitget/RootData](https://www.bitget.com/news/detail/12560605118861) (s); [CoinDesk](https://www.coindesk.com/business/2022/01/31/solana-wallet-phantom-raises-109m-to-rival-metamask) (s)). Hyperliquid's standard pitch against Binance is self-custody, no KYC, hourly funding and lower base fees ([hyperliquidguide](https://hyperliquidguide.com/compare/hyperliquid-vs-binance) (s)).
  - No public competition slide was found for Polymarket, Kalshi, Jupiter, Phantom or Hyperliquid. Hyperliquid raised no outside VC (general knowledge), so it has no deck to cite.

### F6. "Why we win": the wedge plus moat slide, often paired with a landscape
- **What it is.** A compact landscape (or one line naming rivals), then the wedge, the structural reason and the proof.
- **The two-slide version.** Several guides suggest splitting into "landscape and alternatives" followed by "competitive advantages" ([Understory](https://www.understoryagency.com/blog/competitor-analysis-pitch-deck-guide) (s)).
- **When it works.** When you have *evidence* behind the moat, such as win rate, retention, cost per unit or a data loop. At Series A, investors "expect numbers from your CRM: win rate against a named competitor" ([WaveUp](https://waveup.com/blog/how-to-make-a-winning-competition-slide-for-your-pitch-deck/) (s), vendor).
- **When it backfires.**
  - Generic moats: "great team", "first mover", "proprietary AI", "data moat". On data, a16z warns that most "data network effects" are really scale effects ([a16z, Empty Promise of Data Moats](https://a16z.com/the-empty-promise-of-data-moats/) (s)).
  - NFX: "good UX or clever marketing is not sufficient. VCs are looking for technical or really measurable differentiation" (s).
- **Example decks.**
  - **LinkedIn Series B (2004).** Slide 14 casts Ryze as too social, Spoke as too enterprise, and the rest as "Friendster clones". The framing is the teardown's ([startupfundraising.com](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/linkedin-pitch-deck-teardown) (s)).
  - **Coinbase's "Why Coinbase will win"** slide (above).
  - **Kama (pre-seed).** Names Tinder, Bumble and Hinge, then "why we win" and "moat" ([startupfundraising.com](https://startupfundraising.com/library/pitch-deck-examples/slides/competition-slide/by-stage) (s)).

### F7. "Competitors validate the market"
- **What it is.** Rivals' traction or funding used as proof of demand, paired with your wedge. Kawasaki: "You want competition." It "shows, though it doesn't necessarily 'prove,' that the market is attractive" (s).
- **When it works.** As a *supporting line* on F1, F4 or F6, especially when a rival's scale sizes your market.
- **When it backfires.** As a standalone slide it reads as "we're late". It also invites "why won't the leader just add this?"
- **Example decks.**
  - **Airbnb** used Couchsurfing's ~660k members and Craigslist's temporary-housing listings as demand proof. Figures vary by source ([Spectup](https://www.spectup.com/resource-hub/airbnb-pitch-deck-analysis) (s)).
  - **Fibery** compared two categories by market cap (startupfundraising.com (s)).

### Variant: the "ghost", with competition woven through the deck instead
- **Who used it.** Coinbase, Brex, Figma (mostly demo) and MAI (2025 AI-agent seed) had no competition slide (sources above). Underscore names it as the fourth approach.
- **Mixed advice.** An advisor in a First Round piece reportedly says "don't just have a dedicated competition slide" in contested markets, but address competition throughout the deck. I could not confirm the article **?**.
- **When it works.** Only with overwhelming demo or traction, *and* a founder who can handle any rival cold. NFX: "If you are asked about a competitor and need to look it up … your chances of fundraising just went down dramatically" (s).
- **Verdict.** It is not suited to crowded categories.

## 3. What VCs say they hate

1. **"We have no competition."**
   - Kawasaki: it means "you are either addressing a market that doesn't exist or you don't know how to search the internet" (s).
   - Andreessen, via a secondhand repost of a Quora answer: "It's almost always a red flag when someone asserts that they have no competition" ([Goodreads blog](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/13130876-avoid-this-startup-blunder-when-pitching-investors?tab=author) (s)) **?**.
   - Suster: "I hate when I hear companies say [we have no real competition]" (s).
   - Hoffman calls it a myth ([LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/20131015161834-1213-what-i-wish-i-knew-before-pitching-linkedin-to-vcs/) (s)).
2. **Defining the market so narrowly that you win by definition.** Thiel: "Entrepreneurs are always biased to understate the scale of competition, but that is the biggest mistake a startup can make … describ[ing] your market extremely narrowly so that you dominate it by definition." His example is "British food ∩ restaurant ∩ Palo Alto" ([LessWrong summary](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/7LZHS4afrXCNuuGK9/book-summary-zero-to-one) (s)).
3. **Self-graded checkmark grids built on features you chose** (Suster, Extantia, Understory above).
4. **Invented or reverse-engineered axes, and the inevitable top-right placement** (Hunter Walk, Deckary).
5. **Disparaging or belittling rivals.** Suster: "the disdain for your competitor is often felt in the word choices and how you position them" (s). Kawasaki: calling the competition "feeble" fails, because in a meaningful market it isn't (s).
6. **Leaving out the rivals the investor already knows, or indirect players.** NFX: "VCs have probably seen competitors of yours that you haven't" (s). The Tocker partner review flagged exactly this: Ask Gina, Fere AI and Bankr were missing (`../vc-review.md`).
7. **Logo soup with no "why we win"**, or a slide that was out of date before the meeting.
8. **Generic moat claims**: "proprietary AI", a data moat with no loop, "first mover", "execution".

## 4. What makes VCs lean in

1. **Honest acknowledgment, including where rivals are stronger.** Kawasaki asks for your weaknesses. Suster wants you to credit what rivals do well, then say where you differ (s).
2. **The alternative customers actually use today.** That means Sequoia's "indirect" competitors and Dunford's status quo.
3. **A clear wedge.**
   - James Currier (NFX): "you need enough of a wedge to edge out your competition and the incumbents", tied to the long-term story ([NFX](https://www.nfx.com/post/16-fundraising-lessons) (s)).
   - Front's wedge was shared inboxes (hello@, support@).
   - Phantom's wedge was Solana-first.
4. **An insight competitors miss.** YC: "what you know about the problem that everyone else doesn't" (s).
5. **A structural reason incumbents can't follow.** Counter-positioning, pricing model (per-outcome vs per-seat), custody, regulation, or the incumbent's profit pool.
6. **Numbers on every claim.** Examples: win rate vs a named rival, cost per task, retention. NFX: "no sentences without numbers" (s).
7. **A headline that *is* the argument** (NFX), e.g. "The only agent that X, because Y".
8. **Leading a narrower segment.** NFX: being "a 'leader' in a segment, even a narrower one, is better … than being the 2nd or 3rd player in a more crowded field" (s). Dunford also advises owning a sub-category you name rather than creating a new category.
9. **Answering the top objection early.** First Round: rank objections by how often they come up, and answer the top ones in the first 10 minutes ([First Round](https://review.firstround.com/the-fundraising-wisdom-that-helped-our-founders-raise-18b-in-follow-on-capital/) (s)). In a crowded category, "what about X?" *is* the top objection.

### Stage calibration
| Stage | What the slide must carry |
|---|---|
| Pre-seed | Status quo plus a *claimed* difference. Direct rivals may be thin ([startupfundraising.com by-stage](https://startupfundraising.com/library/pitch-deck-examples/slides/competition-slide/by-stage) (s)). |
| Seed | The wedge, the insight, early proof that users switch, and the named rivals the investor knows. |
| Series A | Named head-to-heads, win and loss data, and why the lead compounds. "Category dominance, not parity" (Understory (s)). |
| Series B | Share trend, retention against rivals, and why the moat widens with scale. |

## 5. Crypto and AI-agent companies: crowded and fast-moving

The consensus from the sources, with my synthesis marked.

1. **Assume the partner saw five decks like yours this quarter.**
   - Elad Gil says several AI categories have already "crystallized" around leaders, including coding, legal and support (Decagon, Sierra), while fintech and security remain open ([Elad Blog](https://blog.eladgil.com/p/ai-market-clarity) (s); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/11/03/elad-gil-on-which-ai-markets-have-winners-and-which-are-still-wide-open) (s)).
   - A blog summary of a McKinsey 2026 survey says buyers are cutting to 1–2 agent vendors per workflow ([Sky9](https://www.sky9capital.com/blog/ai-agent-startup-ideas-2026/) (s)) **?**.
   - Name the rivals they know; omitting a famous one costs more than including it.
2. **Put the platform on the slide.**
   - Labs and wallets are moving up the stack and now ship their own agents ([Bessemer State of AI 2025](https://www.bvp.com/atlas/the-state-of-ai-2025) (s)).
   - Investors "almost always ask what happens if OpenAI or Anthropic ships the feature natively". Answer it on the slide, and show **which part of your moat is independent of the base model** ([Runway](https://www.runwayteam.co/post/ai-startup-pitch-deck) (s), vendor).
   - In crypto, the equivalents are Coinbase, Phantom, Binance and the Telegram-bot incumbents.
3. **Speed of building is not a moat. Momentum is evidence.**
   - Quiet Capital's Michael Bloch: "AI compresses the time it takes to do things. It does not compress the time it takes for things to happen" ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/josipamajic/2026/03/31/vcs-rethink-startup-moats-as-ai-compresses-time-to-build/) (s)).
   - a16z's Bryan Kim, on consumer AI: "How do you build a moat in consumer AI? Right now, sorry to say, there is none … what matters is velocity: how fast you can launch, gain traction, and seize mindshare" ([a16z](https://a16z.com/momentum-as-ai-moat/) (s)).
   - **Synthesis:** show shipping cadence and growth as *proof*. Claim the moat that velocity buys you (distribution, data loop, brand), not velocity itself.
4. **In crypto, code is forkable, so moats live elsewhere.**
   - SushiSwap's fork of Uniswap pulled out ~$800M, or more than half of its liquidity.
   - Multicoin: an exchange's defensibility tracks its liquidity, but liquidity can be bought with subsidies ([Multicoin](https://multicoin.capital/2020/04/16/on-forking-defi-protocols/) (s)).
   - The 2025–26 version is "fat distribution": value accrues to whoever owns the user and the order flow ([Mason Nystrom](https://www.masonnystrom.com/p/the-privatization-of-orderflow-and) (s); [Fiebach on X](https://x.com/MattFiebach/status/2001644564772246002) (s)).
   - **So:** name a moat in order flow, distribution, data, trust or record, not "our smart contracts" or "our agent".
5. **Choose axes that are structural, not features.**
   - Good axes are trade-offs rivals would accept: custodial↔self-custody, click-to-trade↔autonomous, generic↔vertical or screened, user pays↔platform pays, private↔public track record, per-seat↔per-outcome pricing.
   - Sky9 draws the line between assistive tools ("crowded") and "agents that own a complete workflow end to end, with accountability for the output" (s).
   - A seed guide warns that vague "agent for X" pitches get screened out by associates ([Causo](https://hub.causo.ai/guides/raising-seed-for-ai-agent-startup-2026) (s), vendor).
6. **Date-stamp the slide and refresh it before every meeting.**
   - Example: Amazon was not on DefinedCrowd's competition slide when it raised, and four years later Amazon shipped "an almost identical product". This comes from a VC write-up that appeared in a search extract; I could not pin down which post (s) **?**.
   - Add an "as of" date, as note 22 already does.
7. **Don't sell alpha on benchmarks. Sell the outcome.** Runway: for application companies, benchmarks are a distraction; show hours saved, revenue or error rate (s). For trading agents, Alpha Arena's losses (note 19) make "our AI trades better" a weak competitive claim.
8. **Use the counter-positioning story when it's true.** Examples: a fee model the incumbent can't drop, a no-custody design a CEX can't adopt, or a public track record that copy-trading platforms can't offer without exposing strategies. Avoid "they're too slow", which is not structural.

## 6. Applying this to Tocker (short)

- **Current state.** The deck's competition slide is an F3 spotlight matrix. The partner review rejected it as self-graded, with a feature column chosen to win (`../vc-review.md`). It proposed an F1 2×2: autonomous↔manual × screened new launches↔generic.
- **A risk in that 2×2.** Using note 22's verified matrix, **Parasol** ("hands-off memecoin agents, rug-filtered") would share Tocker's quadrant, and Fere AI may too. This is the "crowded quadrant" failure.
  - **Option 1:** name the co-occupants in the quadrant and win on a third, structural line, such as "public verified record, private strategy" or "who pays for data".
  - **Option 2:** pick axes where Tocker is really alone, *and* that Parasol would accept as fair.
- **Suggested shape (F6 + F2 + F1). Left side:**
  - A dated landscape grouped by approach: terminals and Telegram bots (Axiom, Trojan); chat agents (Ask Gina, HeyElsa); autonomous agents (Parasol, Fere, Senpi); rails (Bankr, Coinbase).
  - A **status quo** row: "Manual DD in Axiom + Telegram alpha groups".
- **Right side:**
  - A headline with the wedge and one structural reason.
  - One proof number once it exists.
- **Appendix.** Move the verified matrix (note 22) there for diligence, with honest partial marks.
- **Answer "what if Coinbase or Ask Gina ships this?"** on the slide in one line, consistent with the review's question 4.
